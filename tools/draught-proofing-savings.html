The cheapest retrofit on this site

Draught-proofing is the unglamorous champion of home retrofit. A semi-detached home saves roughly £70 a year by sealing its draughts; the DIY materials cost about £120; the payback is 1.7 years; and the comfort change is immediate — no more cold ankles in the living room. No other measure on this site delivers a comparable return per pound, which is why draught-proofing sits at the very top of Phase A in the whole-house retrofit planner. If you do one thing this weekend, make it this.

The test case — semi, all four main measures, DIY at £120 — gives £70 a year and a 1.7-year payback. The professional route at £400 stretches payback to 5.7 years, still respectable, but the DIY case is so easy that paying a professional is rarely necessary unless mobility or time rules it out. Everything below assumes DIY unless stated.

Where draughts actually come from

Rank your draughts before you seal them, because effort should follow impact. Chimneys are usually number one: an open unused fireplace is effectively a 30cm hole in your house, drawing warm air up and out around the clock. A chimney balloon (£20–£30) or a flue blocker, fitted in minutes, is the single highest-ROI item in draught-proofing — but only for genuinely unused fireplaces, and it must be removed (or be the removable type) before any fire is lit. Doors come next: brush strips on the bottom, compression seals around the frame, and a letterbox brush or cover. Windows: self-adhesive foam or rubber compression seals in the frame — a few pounds per window. Floorboards: the gaps between boards in suspended timber floors leak astonishing amounts of air; a tube of flexible sealant or purpose-made beading, worked into the gaps, fixes it in an afternoon per room. Loft hatches are the forgotten one: an unsealed hatch is a hole into a cold void, and warm air rises straight through it — seal the rim and stick insulation on the hatch back.

The candle test finds the rest: on a windy day, hold a lit candle (carefully) near suspect gaps and watch the flame. Sockets and pipework entries on outside walls, the gap under the front door, and service penetrations behind the kitchen units are the usual surprises. Sealant and expanding foam handle the static gaps; brush and compression strips handle the moving ones (doors, opening windows).

Key definitions

Compression seal
A rubber or foam strip that squashes when a door or window closes, sealing the gap. The workhorse of draught-proofing.
Chimney balloon
An inflatable bladder fitted up the flue of an unused fireplace, blocking the updraught. Removable in seconds.
Air brick
A ventilated brick providing deliberate airflow (e.g. to a gas appliance or underfloor void). Never seal these.
Stack effect
Warm air rising through the house and escaping high up, pulling cold air in low down — why loft hatches and chimneys matter most.

The ventilation warning (the most important section on this page)

Sealing draughts is sealing accidental ventilation. Your house also has deliberate ventilation, and the two must never be confused. Never seal: air bricks serving gas appliances or open fires (blocking these can cause carbon-monoxide build-up — this is a safety issue, not an efficiency debate); extractor fans in kitchens and bathrooms; trickle vents in window frames; and vents into underfloor voids (blocking these rots suspended timber floors). The rule is simple: draught-proof the gaps that were never meant to be there — around doors, windows, floorboards, pipework, the loft hatch — and leave alone everything that was installed on purpose.

The condensation corollary: a tighter house holds moisture more. A family produces 10–15 litres of water vapour a day, and in a draughty house much of it escaped with the draughts. After draught-proofing, that moisture needs a managed exit — extractor fans used properly, trickle vents open, lids on pans. If condensation appears on windows after draught-proofing, the answer is ventilation discipline, not un-sealing the draughts. (New double glazing without trickle vents plus enthusiastic draught-proofing is the classic mould recipe — see the glazing calculator's ventilation warning.)

DIY: what to buy and how long it takes

The shopping list for a typical semi: self-adhesive rubber compression seals (£15 for 20m), door brush strips (£8–£12 each), a chimney balloon (£25), flexible floorboard sealant (£8/tube, 3–4 tubes), caulk and a gun (£15), and a letterbox brush (£6). Total: roughly £100–£140, matching the calculator's £120 default. Time: a full Saturday for the whole house, or an hour per measure tackled individually. Skill level: if you can use scissors and a caulk gun, you can do all of it — there is no trade skill involved, which is precisely why the professional route (£400) is hard to justify unless you cannot do the physical work.

Quality notes that matter: buy EPDM rubber seals over cheap foam where you can — foam compresses permanently within a year or two, rubber lasts a decade. For sash windows, brush-pile strips in the staff beads work better than stick-on foam. And for floorboards, do the work in winter when the boards are at their most contracted: seal the maximum gap, and the summer expansion just tightens the seal.

What draught-proofing does not fix

Honest limits. Draught-proofing does not insulate — a sealed single-glazed window still loses heat through the glass, it just stops leaking air around the frame. It does not fix cold walls or cold floors (that is insulation's job). And in a house with serious air leakage through the structure itself — missing mortar, gaps at the wall-ceiling junction — strips and sealant are a partial answer; the full answer is the fabric work in Phase B. Think of draught-proofing as stopping the bleeding: essential, fast, cheap — but not the whole treatment.

Renters: the permission-free retrofit

Draught-proofing is the best retrofit available to renters because almost all of it is reversible and cosmetic: stick-on seals peel off, chimney balloons pull out, brush strips unscrew. No landlord permission is realistically needed for any of it (though telling the landlord costs nothing), and you take the comfort with you in lower bills from day one. For the £120 outlay, a renter in a draughty Victorian terrace gets the same 1.7-year payback as an owner — one of the few efficiency measures where tenure does not matter at all.

Key takeaways

Frequently asked questions

How much does draught-proofing save?

Around £70 a year for a semi-detached home (Energy Saving Trust typical figure), scaled by property size in the calculator above. DIY materials cost ~£120, giving a payback under 2 years.

Can I do draught-proofing myself?

Yes — it is the most DIY-friendly retrofit there is. Self-adhesive seals, brush strips, a chimney balloon and a caulk gun cover nearly everything; a full house takes about a day.

What should I never draught-proof?

Air bricks, extractor fans, trickle vents, and any vent serving a gas appliance or open fire. These are deliberate, safety-critical ventilation — sealing them risks carbon monoxide build-up and serious damp.

Will draught-proofing cause condensation?

It can, if moisture has no managed exit. Use extractor fans, keep trickle vents open, and put lids on pans. Condensation after draught-proofing means ventilate more — not un-seal the draughts.

Is a chimney balloon safe?

Yes for genuinely unused fireplaces — it is removable in seconds. Never use one in a flue that serves a working fire or boiler, and fit a reminder tag so nobody lights a fire with it in place.

Does draught-proofing help with a heat pump?

Enormously. Heat pumps run most efficiently in airtight homes — every draught is heat the pump must replace at 3-4x the cost of gas heat. Draught-proof before sizing any heat pump.
