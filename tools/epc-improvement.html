    How EPC bands and SAP points actually work

    Every EPC is a number first and a letter second. The number is SAP points (Standard Assessment Procedure), scored 1–100, and the letter is just a band drawn around it: A at 92+, B at 81+, C at 69+, D at 55+, E at 39+, F at 21+, G below 21. Two homes both rated "D" can be 14 points apart — one draught-proofing job away from C, or a whole insulation programme away. This is why the estimator works in points, not letters: the letter tells you where you are, the points tell you how far the next letter is and which measures get you there cheapest.

    SAP scores the building, not the occupants. It models the walls, roof, floor, windows, heating system, controls, hot water and lighting against standard occupancy assumptions — the same family behaviour in every house, so the rating compares buildings fairly. That is also its famous limitation: a D-rated house lived in by a frugal household can cost less to run than a C-rated house lived in lavishly. The EPC predicts the building's appetite, not your bill. For retrofit planning that is exactly what you want — you are buying building improvements, and points measure building improvements.

    The point-gain table: what each measure is worth

    The gains in this calculator are SAP-lite estimates — simplified but honest, good for ranking, not for conveyancing:

    • Solid wall insulation +15 pts (~£9,000): the biggest single gain available to pre-1920 homes, because uninsulated solid walls dominate their heat loss. Also the most expensive and disruptive measure on the list.
    • Heat pump +12 pts (~£4,500 net of BUS): the biggest systems gain — SAP rewards the efficiency leap from burning gas at 85% to moving heat at 300%+.
    • Cavity wall insulation +10 pts (~£1,000): the value champion for 1930s–1980s homes. Ten points — most of a band — for a day's work.
    • Loft insulation to 270mm +8 pts (~£500): eight points for the cheapest fabric measure in Britain. Almost always the first tick.
    • Solar PV +6 pts (~£6,400): SAP credits the generation. Modest points, but the bill saving is the real prize.
    • Double glazing +5 pts (~£5,000): five points for five thousand pounds — the table makes the poor value visible, which is the point.
    • Heating controls +3, draught-proofing +2, cylinder jacket +1: the small stuff. Individually trivial in points, collectively a band nudge — and the cheapest pounds you'll spend.

    Notice what the table teaches: points and payback are different currencies. The cylinder jacket is the best payback on this site and worth 1 point; solid wall insulation is the most points and a 15-year payback. The EPC Improvement Estimator ranks by points per pound because its job is the rating; pair it with the Retrofit Planner, which ranks by payback, and you get both lenses on the same house.

    Key definitions

    SAP points
    Standard Assessment Procedure score, 1–100. The number behind the EPC letter.
    EPC band
    The A–G letter drawn around SAP points (A 92+, B 81+, C 69+, D 55+, E 39+, F 21+, G <21).
    RdSAP
    Reduced-data SAP — the simplified survey method used for existing homes (vs full SAP for new builds).
    Points per £1k
    This estimator's value metric: SAP points gained per £1,000 spent. Higher is better value for the rating.

    The test case: D to C for £1,500

    The spec's example is the most common EPC journey in Britain: a D-rated home at 60 points adds loft insulation (+8) and cavity wall fill (+10) to reach 78 points — band C, for about £1,500. That is not cherry-picked; it is the modal outcome for the 1930s–1970s semi, and it explains why "EPC C by 2030" targets keep centring on these two measures. The calculator shows the same arithmetic for your home: enter your points, tick the measures, and watch the projected band move — then check the cost-per-point to see whether the next band is £1,500 away or £15,000 away. That distance is the single most useful number in retrofit planning, because it separates the homes one winter's work fixes from the homes that need a programme.

    When the rating actually matters (legally)

    For most of this site's readers the EPC is a planning tool. But it has legal teeth in three places, and the estimator's caveat exists for them: renting out a property requires a valid EPC of E or above (with C targeted in future regulation — check current rules), and only an accredited assessor's certificate counts; selling requires a valid EPC on listing; and grant eligibility (ECO4, GBIS) keys off the EPC band on record. In all three cases this calculator's projection is inadmissible — useful for deciding what to do, useless for proving you did it. After the work, pay for the real assessment (£60–£120): the uplift it documents is part of the return, capitalised into the property value.

    Gaming the rating vs improving the home

    A word of professional caution, because the point-gain table tempts it: SAP points are gameable in ways that don't help the house. Low-energy lighting swaps, a new boiler in an uninsulated shell, or the cheapest possible controls can nudge points without touching the building's real appetite. The estimator ranks honestly, but use it as a compass, not a target — the goal is a warmer, cheaper, lower-carbon home, and the letter follows the building. The measures that score best on points-per-pound (cylinder jacket: ~29 pts/£1k; draught-proofing: ~13 pts/£1k; loft: 16 pts/£1k) also happen to be genuinely good building improvements, which is reassuring: on this table, at least, the rating and the reality mostly agree.

    Reading your EPC certificate properly

    If you have a certificate already — every home sold or rented since 2008 should — read past the letter before you plan. Page two lists the building's features each rated one to five stars: walls, roof, windows, heating, controls, hot water, lighting. The one-star items are your shopping list, and they usually confirm what the estimator suggests: “walls: very poor” plus “roof: poor” in a 1960s semi is the loft-and-cavity story in official language. Page three is the goldmine most owners ignore: the assessor's recommended measures, each with an indicative cost range and the typical saving. These recommendations are generated from the same RdSAP model, so they should broadly agree with this estimator's ranking — where they disagree, trust the assessor's (it saw your actual house).

    Two traps in reading certificates: first, the “potential rating” on page one assumes you do all the recommended measures, including the uneconomic ones — treat it as a ceiling, not a promise. Second, certificates expire after ten years, and a 2016 certificate predates your 2021 boiler or your 2023 glazing; the register (searchable free on gov.uk) may be scoring a house you no longer own, thermally speaking. If you've done major work since the assessment, a fresh EPC (£60–£120) is cheap documentation of the improvement — and the number it prints is the one buyers, tenants and grant schemes will actually use.

    Putting it together with the planner

    Use the two tools as a pair. The EPC Improvement Estimator answers "what moves my rating cheapest?" — the compliance and value question. The Retrofit Planner answers "what do I do first?" — the sequencing question. A landlord facing EPC-C regulation runs the estimator first (minimum spend to compliance), then the planner (sensible order to do it in). An owner-occupier chasing bills runs the planner first, then checks the estimator to see what the rating does along the way — a pleasant surprise at sale time, when the documented C where a D used to be is worth real money.

    Key takeaways

    • Always size the measure for your home before spending — this calculator gives you the numbers.
    • Compare like-for-like quotes from certified installers and check live prices before you commit.
    • Fabric first: insulation and draught-proofing make every later upgrade (heat pumps, solar) cheaper and more effective.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many SAP points is each EPC band?

    A: 92+, B: 81+, C: 69+, D: 55+, E: 39+, F: 21+, G: below 21. The letter is just a band around the points — two 'D' homes can be 14 points apart.

    Which measure improves EPC rating the most per pound?

    The cylinder jacket (~29 pts per £1,000) and draught-proofing (~13 pts/£1k) are the cheapest points; loft insulation gives 8 points for ~£500. Solid wall insulation gives the most points (+15) but costs ~£9,000.

    Can I go from EPC D to C cheaply?

    Often yes: D at 60 points plus loft insulation (+8) and cavity wall fill (+10) reaches 78 points — band C — for about £1,500 in a typical semi. That is the most common EPC journey in Britain.

    Is this estimator the same as a real EPC assessment?

    No. It is a planning estimate using simplified SAP-lite gains. Only an accredited assessor's RdSAP survey (£60-£120) produces a legally valid EPC for sales, rentals and grant eligibility.

    Do I need a minimum EPC to rent out my property?

    Currently E or above for most rentals in England and Wales, with tighter standards proposed — check the current regulations. Only a valid EPC certificate counts, not an estimate.

    Should I chase EPC points or bill savings?

    Both, with different tools: this estimator ranks by points-per-pound (the rating question); the Retrofit Planner ranks by payback (the bills question). The cheapest points and the fastest paybacks are often the same measures.
