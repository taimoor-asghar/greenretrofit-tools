What a "green tariff" actually means

Every UK supplier can sell you a "100% renewable" tariff, and the phrase means something narrower than it sounds. When you switch to a green tariff, the electrons arriving at your meter are the same grid mix as your neighbour's — what changes is the paperwork: for each kWh you use, your supplier buys a REGO (Renewable Energy Guarantee of Origin) certificate proving a kWh of renewable electricity was generated somewhere. The system works, broadly, but it has a known weakness: REGOs are cheap, so a supplier can buy dirty wholesale power plus cheap certificates and market the result as green. This is why informed buyers look past the headline and ask what the supplier actually buys — direct power-purchase agreements with wind and solar farms, or just certificates on the open market.

That distinction is the difference between tariffs that genuinely fund new renewables and tariffs that relabel the status quo. The deepest green tariffs — typically from suppliers that own generation or sign long-term PPAs — cost a little more and do a little more. Certificate-only green tariffs cost about the same as standard deals and do about the same for the climate. Neither is a scam; they are just different products, and the marketing rarely says which one you are buying. The renewable % column in the calculator is your prompt to check: 100% REGO-backed is the claim; the supplier's fuel-mix disclosure (published annually, find it on their site) is the evidence.

The comparison maths: unit rate is not the price

The single most expensive mistake in tariff comparison is ranking by unit rate alone. Your annual cost is kWh × unit rate + standing charge × 365, and the standing charge — the flat daily fee for being connected — can flip the ranking. The spec's test case shows it: 2,900 kWh at 24p/kWh with a 55p/day standing charge costs 2,900 × 0.24 + 0.55 × 365 = £696 + £200.75 = £896.75 a year. A "cheaper" 23p tariff with a 60p standing charge costs 2,900 × 0.23 + 0.60 × 365 = £667 + £219 = £886 — a saving of only £10.75 despite the headline rate looking a penny better. Meanwhile a 23p tariff with a 50p standing charge costs £849.50, saving £47.25. Same unit rate, £37 a year apart — the standing charge did all the work.

This is why the calculator forces you to enter both numbers for every tariff, and why its ranking is by total annual cost, never by unit rate. Low users (under ~2,000 kWh) should weight the standing charge heavily — it can be a third of their bill. High users should weight the unit rate. And everyone should re-run the numbers with their actual usage from a bill, not the Ofgem typical 2,700 kWh, because a 20% usage error moves the answer more than most tariff differences.

Key definitions

Unit rate (p/kWh)
What you pay per kilowatt-hour used. The headline number — but only half the price.
Standing charge (p/day)
Flat daily fee for grid connection, paid even if you use nothing. £200+/yr at current levels.
REGO
Renewable Energy Guarantee of Origin — the certificate suppliers buy to back "100% renewable" claims.
kWh-matched
A tariff where the supplier buys renewable certificates (or power) equal to your usage — the minimum credible green claim.
PPA
Power Purchase Agreement — a long-term contract directly with a wind/solar farm. The gold standard behind a green tariff.

Why the example rates are examples (read this)

The three tariffs prefilled in the calculator are illustrative only — realistic shapes (a fixed deal, a variable deal, a time-of-use deal) with made-up numbers. This is deliberate, and the page says so three times, because presenting example rates as live would be misinformation the moment wholesale prices move — which is monthly. Energy is the fastest-moving price in the household budget: a tariff that was cheapest in spring can be mid-pack by autumn.

So use this tool to learn the method — total annual cost, both charges, renewable % — and then take that method to an Ofgem-accredited comparison site for live quotes. The accreditation matters: accredited sites must show all tariffs, not just the ones that pay commission, and must use your actual usage. Get three live quotes, plug them into the calculator's table, and let the ranked output tell you the truth. Never switch on a headline unit rate seen in an advert.

When green costs more — and when it doesn't

The honest picture in 2026: genuinely additional green tariffs (PPA-backed, supplier-owned generation) typically cost £20–£60 a year more than the cheapest standard variable deal for a typical home. Certificate-only green tariffs are usually within a few pounds of standard. Time-of-use green tariffs (cheap overnight, pricier daytime) can be the cheapest of all if you can shift load — EV charging, hot water, dishwasher and washing machine into the cheap window. A household with an EV on an intelligent overnight tariff can cut electricity costs 30–50% versus a flat rate, and the electrons at 2am are disproportionately wind-powered anyway.

The trap to avoid is the "green premium" with no green content: a tariff priced above standard that is REGO-only. Check the supplier's fuel mix disclosure and, if you want the gold standard, whether they publish what fraction of their renewable claims comes from PPAs versus certificates. A few pounds a year extra for a PPA-backed tariff is arguably the cheapest carbon offset a household can buy — but only if the pounds actually reach a wind farm.

Fixed vs variable: the switching decision

Beyond greenness, the calculator helps with the oldest tariff question: fix or stay variable? A fixed tariff locks unit rate and standing charge for 12–24 months, usually at a small premium over the current variable rate. It wins if wholesale prices rise (you are insulated) and loses if they fall (you are stuck, minus any exit fee — check it, typically £30–£75 per fuel). The variable tariff tracks the Ofgem price cap, moving quarterly. The rule of thumb: fix when the fixed quote is within ~5% of the variable and you value certainty; stay variable when fixed quotes carry a big premium, which usually means the market expects prices to fall. Either way, the decision input is total annual cost from the calculator — not the marketing headline.

A final note on switching itself

Switching supplier is deliberately frictionless by regulation: it completes within five working days, your supply never drops, and any credit balance transfers automatically. The only real switching risk is exit fees on fixed deals (£30–£75 per fuel), which is why the calculator's ranked table matters more than any advert — run your actual usage through it, read the renewable % column sceptically, and switch on total annual cost. Done once a year, this ten-minute exercise is worth £40–£100 for the typical household, every year, with zero lifestyle change.

Beyond the tariff: cutting the kWh themselves

A final uncomfortable truth: the greenest tariff on a wasteful home is still a wasteful home. Every kWh you don't use needs no certificate, no PPA, no debate about additionality — it is simply not generated. Before (or alongside) switching, run the home through the quick wins on this site: standby (£40–£70/yr), LEDs (£230/yr for the classic halogen swap), draught-proofing (£70/yr), the cylinder jacket (£85/yr). A home that cuts usage 20% and then switches to a genuinely green tariff has done more for the climate than a home that switches tariffs and changes nothing — and the bill shows it twice.

Frequently asked questions

Are green tariffs actually better for the environment?

It depends on what backs them. Tariffs backed by direct power-purchase agreements (PPAs) with wind/solar farms genuinely fund renewables. REGO-certificate-only tariffs are usually no worse than standard but add little new renewable generation. Check the supplier's fuel-mix disclosure.

Why do the calculator's tariffs say 'example'?

Because energy rates change monthly — any fixed numbers would be wrong within weeks. The three tariffs are realistic shapes (fixed, variable, time-of-use) for learning the comparison method. Always get live quotes from an Ofgem-accredited comparison site before switching.

Should I compare by unit rate or total cost?

Total annual cost, always: kWh × unit rate + standing charge × 365. A 23p tariff with a 60p/day standing charge can cost £37/yr more than another 23p tariff with a 50p standing charge at 2,900 kWh.

Is a fixed or variable tariff better right now?

Fix when the fixed quote is within ~5% of the variable rate and you value certainty; stay variable when fixed quotes carry a big premium (the market usually expects prices to fall then). Check exit fees (£30-£75 per fuel) before fixing.

What is a time-of-use tariff?

A tariff with cheap overnight rates and pricier daytime rates (e.g. for EV owners). It can be the cheapest option if you can shift big loads — EV charging, hot water, laundry — into the cheap window.

Does switching supplier interrupt my supply?

No. Switching is a billing change only; the same wires and meter stay in place, and Ofgem rules mean the switch completes within 5 working days with no gap in supply.
