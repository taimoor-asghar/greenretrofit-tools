    What your home carbon footprint actually includes

    A carbon footprint is an estimate of the greenhouse gases your lifestyle produces in a year, measured in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e). This calculator focuses on the parts you can actually change with retrofit decisions: home heating, electricity, driving, and flights, plus a diet component for completeness. It deliberately leaves out things like clothing and imported goods, because those are dominated by consumption choices rather than the building — and the whole point of this site is what to do to your house.

    The UK average is roughly 10 tonnes per person per year when everything is counted, which makes the UK one of the higher-footprint countries in Europe. The Climate Change Committee's pathway to net zero by 2050 requires that to fall to around 2–3 tonnes per person. Your home's heating and hot water are typically the single biggest controllable chunk: a gas-heated semi using 12,000 kWh a year produces about 2.5 tonnes from heating alone — a quarter of the national average from one boiler.

    The emission factors behind the maths

    Every figure in this calculator comes from published UK conversion factors, principally the government's annually updated greenhouse gas conversion factors. Natural gas is 0.21 kg CO₂ per kWh — this figure barely moves year to year because burning gas is burning gas. Grid electricity is 0.207 kg CO₂ per kWh, and this one matters: it has fallen dramatically as coal left the grid and wind and solar grew, which is exactly why electrifying your heating (a heat pump) and your car (an EV) gets more carbon-effective every year. Ten years ago an EV barely beat a diesel on carbon; today it wins by a mile, and the gap widens as the grid greens.

    For cars we use per-mile factors: petrol 0.28, diesel 0.27, hybrid 0.20, EV on the UK grid 0.05 kg CO₂ per mile. These are tailpipe-plus-electricity figures, not full lifecycle numbers — manufacturing an EV battery adds roughly 2–4 tonnes upfront, which the car typically "pays back" within 15,000–20,000 miles of driving on a green grid. Flights are the roughest numbers in the calculator: 0.25 tonnes for a short-haul return, 1.0 tonne for a long-haul return, without the extra radiative-forcing multiplier some methodologies apply. If anything, these understate flying's impact.

    Key definitions

    CO₂e
    Carbon dioxide equivalent — all greenhouse gases converted to the warming power of CO₂.
    Emission factor
    How much CO₂e each unit of energy produces (e.g. 0.21 kg per kWh of gas).
    Scope of this calculator
    Direct home energy, personal transport and flights. Excludes food supply chains beyond the diet adder, clothing, and services.
    Net zero (home)
    A home whose remaining emissions are balanced by removals — in practice, a heat pump + green electricity + good fabric.

    Where the tonnes actually hide

    Run the test case — 12,000 kWh gas, 3,000 kWh electricity, 8,000 petrol miles — and you get 2.52 + 0.62 + 2.24 = 5.38 tonnes before flights and diet. Heating and the car dominate; electricity is small because UK electricity is relatively clean and most homes do not use that much of it. This is the most useful insight the calculator gives you: the hierarchy of your personal emissions is almost always heating first, car second, flights third (if you fly), everything else a distant fourth.

    That hierarchy is why this site's retrofit order is fabric, then heating, then everything else. Loft and cavity insulation cut the gas number directly. A heat pump replaces the 0.21 gas factor with the 0.207 electricity factor divided by a SCOP of 3–4 — roughly a 70–80% cut in heating emissions overnight. An EV on an overnight tariff does the same for the car number. These are not marginal lifestyle tweaks; they are the two decisions that move the needle most for a typical UK household.

    What the calculator can't tell you

    Honest limits. The diet figures are the coarsest in the tool: the difference between a meat-heavy and a vegan diet is roughly 1.5 tonnes a year in the academic literature, but it varies enormously with how much you eat and waste. The flight numbers ignore the extra warming effect of emissions at altitude, which some researchers put at 2–3 times the CO₂ alone — our figures are conservative. And the whole calculator is annual and static: it does not model the grid getting cleaner, which means a heat pump or EV installed today gets better on carbon every year it runs. If anything, the calculator slightly understates the case for electrification.

    The "one big thing" rule

    Behavioural research keeps finding the same thing: people who try to change twenty small habits change none of them, while people who make one big structural change — heat pump, EV, insulation, ditching a commute — stick with it. The calculator's ranked actions follow this logic. Do not start by agonising over phone chargers; start with the biggest bar on your personal chart and work down. One structural change beats a year of virtuous small ones.

    Carbon offsets: the uncomfortable truth

    You will be offered offsets for your flights and your footprint, usually at £10–£25 a tonne. Some are legitimate — verified removals or avoided emissions with proper additionality. Many are not: forestry projects that would have happened anyway, or credits sold twice. The hierarchy is always reduce first, offset only what remains, and treat cheap offsets with deep scepticism. Nothing you buy for £15 undoes a tonne of jet fuel with certainty. This calculator ranks reductions, not offsets, deliberately.

    Year-on-year: tracking your footprint down

    A footprint is most useful as a baseline, not a verdict. Re-run this calculator once a year — same inputs, honest numbers — and you will see which changes actually moved the dial. Typical UK trajectories look like this: insulation and draught-proofing shave 10–20% off the gas number in year one; a heat pump cuts the heating number by 70–80% in the year it is installed; an EV on an overnight tariff does the same for the car number; and each avoided long-haul flight removes a full tonne in a single decision. Households that make the two big electrification moves commonly halve their footprint within three years, which is a pace the national average has never managed.

    Keep your workings. Screenshot the result page, note the date, and store it with your energy bills. If you later sell the house, a documented falling footprint alongside an improving EPC is a genuinely persuasive package for buyers — estate agents increasingly lead with running costs, and "heating bills halved since 2026" is a line that lands. The number also disciplines spending: when a salesman offers you a £6,000 battery "for the planet", your footprint tells you whether your electricity number is even big enough for the battery to matter. Usually it is not — see our battery payback calculator for the blunt version.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is a good carbon footprint for a UK home?

    The UK average is roughly 10 tonnes per person per year across everything. A well-insulated home with a heat pump, an EV and no flying can get under 4 tonnes. The Climate Change Committee's net-zero pathway implies ~2-3 tonnes per person by 2050.

    Why is my heating such a big part of my footprint?

    Because natural gas emits 0.21 kg CO₂ per kWh and UK homes use a lot of it — typically 11,500 kWh a year. A heat pump cuts heating emissions by 70-80% because it moves heat rather than making it, and UK electricity keeps getting cleaner.

    Are the emission factors in this calculator up to date?

    They are based on the UK government's published greenhouse gas conversion factors. The electricity factor (0.207) falls most years as the grid decarbonises, so electrification (heat pumps, EVs) gets better on carbon every year.

    Do I need to include flights? I only fly once a year.

    One long-haul return is about 1 tonne — a tenth of the UK average from a single trip. Include it honestly; for frequent flyers it is often the single biggest item, which is why the calculator ranks it.

    Should I buy carbon offsets for my footprint?

    Reduce first, offset only what remains. Offset quality varies wildly — some projects are verified and additional, many are not. Cheap offsets do not reliably undo a tonne of emissions.

    Does switching to a green electricity tariff really cut my footprint?

    It cuts the carbon attributed to your electricity to near zero on paper, and it signals demand for renewables. But the grid is shared: the deeper win is using less and electrifying heating and transport so the clean grid does more work.
