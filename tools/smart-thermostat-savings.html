Reality check:

What smart thermostats actually do (and don't do)

A smart thermostat does not make your boiler more efficient. It cannot change the laws of thermodynamics, whatever the box claims. What it does is stop you heating an empty house, overheating rooms you are not using, and running the heating on a rigid timer that bears no relation to your life. The savings come entirely from behaviour the device automates: turning down when you leave, learning when you return, and — in the better models — adjusting for the weather so the boiler works less hard on mild days.

The test case — a £900 heating bill, no real controls, empty on weekdays — saves £162 a year (18%), paying back a £200 device in 1.2 years. That is the good end of the range, and it is realistic for the millions of UK homes still running a boiler on a basic timer with no room thermostat. At the other end, a home with a thermostat, programmer and thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs) already fitted might save 2–3% — £20–£30 a year, a seven-year payback. The calculator is honest about which end you are at, because the marketing never is.

Which smart features genuinely save money

Not all "smart" features are equal. The ones with real evidence behind them: geofencing (the heating drops when the last phone leaves and recovers before you return — kills the "forgot to turn it off" waste), learning schedules (adapts to your actual pattern rather than the timer you set in 2019 and never touched), and weather compensation / load compensation (adjusts flow temperature to the weather, so condensing boilers actually condense — this one saves gas even when you are home). Multi-room control with smart TRVs extends the saving to individual rooms: heat the office, not the spare bedroom.

The features that are mostly marketing: voice control (convenient, saves nothing), energy-history dashboards (interesting for a week), and "AI optimisation" claims without weather compensation underneath. Buy for geofencing, scheduling flexibility, and boiler modulation — the rest is garnish.

Key definitions

TRV
Thermostatic radiator valve — the numbered dial on each radiator that limits its individual temperature. Cheap (£10–£15 each) and effective.
Geofencing
The thermostat tracks household phones' location and adjusts heating when everyone leaves / someone returns.
Weather compensation
The boiler lowers its flow temperature on mild days, burning less gas. Needs a compatible boiler and an outdoor sensor or internet weather data.
Load compensation
Similar, but based on the gap between room temperature and target rather than outside weather. OpenTherm boilers do this well.

Compatibility: combi, system and heat-pump homes

Most smart thermostats work with combi boilers and system/regular boilers — the wiring is standard, and self-install takes under an hour if you are comfortable with basic electrics (mains off, obviously). The compatibility questions that matter: hot water control — if you have a hot water cylinder (system/regular boiler), you want a model that controls hot water timing too, not just heating; OpenTherm — some boilers speak this modulation protocol, letting the thermostat fine-tune output rather than just switching on/off, which is measurably more efficient; and heat pumps — these need thermostats that understand low, steady temperatures, not on/off blasts. A smart thermostat designed for boilers can actually fight a heat pump's efficiency by demanding rapid warm-ups. If you have or plan a heat pump, check the heat-pump compatibility list before buying.

Electric storage heaters and underfloor electric heating are separate worlds with their own controls — a standard smart thermostat is not the answer there. And in flats with communal heating, you often cannot fit one at all; check with the building manager.

The £15 alternative nobody advertises

Before spending £200, consider what £30 of TRVs achieves. Fitting thermostatic valves to every radiator — a DIY-able job — lets each room hold its own temperature, which captures a large share of the "don't heat empty rooms" saving that smart systems sell. A £15 plug-in programmable thermostat on the wall does the scheduling. The honest hierarchy: TRVs first (£30–£60, huge bang for buck), a decent programmable thermostat second, a smart thermostat third for the automation layer. Many homes should stop at step two. The calculator's "weak case" verdict exists precisely for homes at step two already.

Installation and running costs

Professional installation runs £150–£250 including the device; self-install kits cost £120–£180. Most models need a constant power supply at the thermostat location — battery-only models exist but chew through batteries. Ongoing costs are nil beyond the app (no subscriptions on the mainstream models as of 2026, though check — a couple of brands have experimented with premium tiers). Compatibility with your boiler brand matters more than the feature list: check the manufacturer's compatibility checker with your exact boiler model before ordering, not after.

Data and privacy: the fine print

A smart thermostat knows when your house is empty. That is the entire point of geofencing, and it is also a dataset worth thinking about. The mainstream brands' privacy policies allow aggregated, anonymised usage analytics; check whether yours opts you into anything beyond that, and whether location data is stored or processed on-device. This is not a reason to avoid the technology — it is a reason to read the settings screen once, turn off what you do not need, and pick a brand with a clear policy.

Where smart controls sit in the retrofit order

Controls are a Phase A quick win: cheap, fast payback (for poorly controlled homes), and zero disruption. But they are not a substitute for fabric. A smart thermostat in a draughty, uninsulated house is an efficient manager of waste — it will dutifully minimise the heating of rooms that leak heat through single glazing. Do the energy audit first, insulate the loft (£300/year saving for £500), draught-proof (£70/year for £120 DIY), and then let the smart thermostat optimise what remains. Controls multiply the value of fabric; they do not replace it.

Key takeaways

  • Always size the measure for your home before spending — this calculator gives you the numbers.

  • Fabric first: insulation and draught-proofing make every later upgrade (heat pumps, solar) cheaper and more effective.

Frequently asked questions

How much can a smart thermostat really save?

It depends entirely on your current controls: 10-18% for homes with no real controls, 5-10% with a basic thermostat, 2-5% if you already have a thermostat plus TRVs. The calculator above estimates your position honestly.

Do smart thermostats work with combi boilers?

Yes — combis are the most compatible boiler type, and self-install typically takes under an hour. Check your exact boiler model on the manufacturer's compatibility checker first.

Are smart thermostats compatible with heat pumps?

Some are, some actively harm efficiency. Heat pumps need low, steady temperatures — a boiler-style thermostat demanding rapid warm-ups fights the heat pump. Buy a model with explicit heat-pump support if relevant.

Do I need smart TRVs on every radiator too?

Not necessarily. Standard TRVs (£10-15 each) capture most of the room-by-room saving. Smart TRVs add app control and scheduling per room — nice, but the payback stretches.

Is geofencing worth it?

It is the single most effective smart feature for households with irregular schedules — it eliminates the 'heated an empty house all day' waste that rigid timers cause.

Will a smart thermostat pay for itself?

For poorly controlled homes, usually within 1-2 years. For homes with a thermostat and TRVs already, treat it as a £200 comfort upgrade rather than an investment.
