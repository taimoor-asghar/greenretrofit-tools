The blunt maths first

A solar battery earns its keep one way: it stores surplus daytime solar that you would otherwise export cheaply, and lets you use it in the evening instead of buying expensive grid electricity. Each stored kWh is therefore worth the spread between your import and export tariffs — at 24p import and 8p export, 16p per kWh. The test case (4,200 kWh generation, 40% self-consumption, 10 kWh battery at £5,000) stores an extra 1,512 kWh a year, saving £241.92 — a 20.7-year payback on a battery with a 10-year warranty. Read that again: the battery wears out roughly twice before it pays for itself. This is the normal case in 2026, not a pessimistic one, and it is why this calculator leads with a blunt verdict instead of a sales pitch.

The two numbers that move the verdict are the tariff spread and the battery price. Widen the spread — say 30p import on a standard tariff against 4p export — and the same battery saves £393 a year: still a 12.7-year payback, better but not good. Halve the battery price (which is roughly where the industry needs to get) and the maths transforms. Until then, batteries are a lifestyle and resilience purchase wearing an investment costume. Nothing wrong with that — but buy it for the right reasons.

Why the export tariff is the battery's enemy

This surprises people: a good export tariff makes batteries worse value. Every kWh the battery stores is a kWh you do not export, so at a 15p SEG rate (Octopus Intelligent Flux and similar), storing a kWh earns you 24p of avoided import but costs 15p of forgone export — a 9p net gain instead of 16p. The battery's value is the spread, and generous export tariffs compress it. There is a genuine strategic question here: with a top-tier export tariff, you may earn more by exporting everything and buying cheap overnight electricity on an EV tariff than by storing at all. Run both scenarios. The battery industry rarely mentions this because it undermines the upsell.

Key definitions

Usable capacity
The kWh you can actually draw — less than nominal capacity because batteries protect a reserve. Always compare usable figures.
Depth of discharge (DoD)
How far the battery is allowed to drain. 90–95% is typical for lithium; the protected remainder extends life.
Cycles
One full charge-discharge. The calculator assumes ~300 useful full cycles/year — UK solar simply does not fill a battery daily in winter.
SEG (Smart Export Guarantee)
Payment for exported electricity. High SEG rates shrink the battery's value — see above.

Battery degradation: the warranty vs reality

Lithium batteries degrade: expect roughly 70–80% of original capacity remaining at year 10, which is what the standard 10-year warranty guarantees (usually "70% capacity or 10 years, whichever comes first", sometimes with a throughput cap in MWh). The calculator's payback comparison against the warranty is deliberately stark — a 20-year payback on a 10-year-warrantied product means you are buying two batteries to earn one battery's return. Degradation also erodes the saving year by year: the £242 first-year saving is maybe £200 by year 8. Real-world UK data is still thin (domestic batteries only took off around 2022–23), so treat manufacturer curves with the scepticism due to any marketing graph.

Technology notes: nearly all domestic batteries in 2026 are lithium iron phosphate (LFP) — safer and longer-lived than the older NMC chemistry, at the cost of slightly lower energy density (irrelevant in a garage). Avoid anything not LFP for a new purchase. Sizing: a 10 kWh battery covers a typical evening's use (cooking, TV, lights — roughly 5–8 kWh for most households); bigger batteries spend winter half-empty, earning nothing on their extra capacity. Oversizing is the most common battery mistake after buying one at all.

When batteries DO make sense

The honest buy-list. High import tariffs: if you are on 30p+ standard rates with no EV tariff option, the spread justifies more storage. Frequent outages: parts of rural Britain lose power regularly; a battery with backup-gateway functionality keeps lights, heating controls and the fridge running. (Check the backup actually works in an outage — some "battery" installs are grid-tied only and shut down with the grid for safety.) Time-of-use arbitrage without solar: charging a battery on 7p overnight electricity and discharging through the 30p evening peak earns ~£1 per full cycle — £300+ a year for a 10 kWh unit cycled daily, no panels needed. This is arguably the best battery economics in Britain right now, and it does not require a roof. Energy independence as a value: some buyers simply want less exposure to the grid and price shocks. That is a legitimate preference — just price it as consumption, not investment.

Installation realities

Retrofitting a battery to existing solar needs an AC-coupled unit (works with any existing inverter) or a hybrid-inverter swap; new installs usually pair a hybrid inverter with a DC-coupled battery at slightly better efficiency. Location matters: batteries prefer cool, dry, ventilated spaces — garages are ideal, hot lofts are not, and some manufacturers void warranties above 30°C ambient. Fire safety: LFP is far less fire-prone than NMC, but building guidance increasingly asks for separation from escape routes — your installer should site it accordingly. And check the 0% VAT position: batteries installed alongside solar are zero-rated; standalone retrofit batteries have had murkier VAT treatment — confirm on the quote.

One more consideration: grid services. Some tariffs and trials pay battery owners for grid flexibility — letting the grid draw from your battery at peak times for a fee. Payments are modest today (£50–£150 a year in most trials) but the market is growing, and a battery enrolled in flexibility schemes earns a second income stream the calculator does not model. Ask your supplier what is available; it will not transform the payback, but it shortens it.

Second-life and second-hand batteries

A word on the bargain end of the market: used EV batteries repurposed for home storage are appearing at eye-catching prices — sometimes under £2,000 for 10+ kWh. The appeal is obvious and the risks are real. A second-life battery has unknown history: how hard it was cycled, whether it was ever thermally stressed, how much capacity genuinely remains. Reputable repurposers test, grade and re-warrant their packs (typically 5 years), and at half the price of new the maths can genuinely work — a £2,500 second-life unit saving £242 a year pays back in about 10 years, inside its warranty. The cowboys sell untested packs with a handshake. If the price looks too good, ask for the test report, the grading certificate, and the warranty in writing — and check your home insurance position, because some insurers ask questions about non-certified storage. For most buyers, new LFP with a 10-year manufacturer warranty remains the sane choice; second-life is for the technically confident who can evaluate what they are buying.

The coming price crash (maybe)

Battery pack prices have fallen ~90% since 2010, and analysts expect continued declines as manufacturing scales — but UK installed prices have been sticky, because the hardware is only half the cost: scaffolding-free half-day installs, DNO paperwork, and installer margins move slower than cell prices. The rational strategy for most households: wait. Your solar earns its export tariff today; battery prices are likelier to be lower in three years than higher. The exception is if you need backup power now, or your tariff spread is exceptional. Patience is a legitimate battery strategy — and it is free.

Key takeaways

  • Always size the measure for your home before spending — this calculator gives you the numbers.
  • Compare like-for-like quotes from certified installers and check live prices before you commit.
  • Fabric first: insulation and draught-proofing make every later upgrade (heat pumps, solar) cheaper and more effective.

Frequently asked questions

Do solar batteries pay for themselves?

Rarely at 2026 prices. A typical £5,000, 10 kWh battery saves ~£240/year — a ~20-year payback against a 10-year warranty. Buy for backup power, high tariffs or independence — not as an investment.

Does a high export tariff make a battery less worthwhile?

Yes. The battery earns the spread between import and export tariffs, so a generous SEG rate (e.g. 15p) compresses the saving. With top export tariffs, exporting everything can beat storing.

What size battery do I need?

Match it to your evening use: ~10 kWh covers most households' evenings. Bigger batteries sit half-empty through winter, earning nothing on the extra capacity. Oversizing is the most common battery mistake.

Can I get a battery without solar panels?

Yes — charge on a cheap overnight tariff (7p) and discharge through the evening peak. At ~£1 per full cycle for a 10 kWh unit, time-of-use arbitrage can be the best battery economics available.

How long do solar batteries last?

Expect 70-80% capacity at 10 years (the standard warranty term). Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) is the chemistry to buy in 2026 — safer and longer-lived than older NMC.

Will battery prices fall?

Cell prices keep falling but UK installed prices are sticky — labour, paperwork and margins move slowly. Waiting a few years is a rational strategy unless you need backup now.
