    The £43.80 question

    Take four utterly ordinary devices — a TV (3W on standby), a set-top box (8W), a games console (10W) and a printer (4W). None of them is doing anything. Add them up: 25 watts, sitting there doing nothing, 20 hours a day. The maths is unforgiving: 25 × 20 × 365 ÷ 1,000 = 182.5 kWh a year, and at 24p per kWh that is £43.80 — every single year — for literally nothing. No light, no heat, no entertainment. That is the number this calculator exists to make visible, because standby waste is invisible by design: it never appears on a bill as a line item, it just silently inflates the total.

    Scale it to the whole house and the typical British home wastes £40–£70 a year on standby, with games-console households often north of £100. The Energy Saving Trust has long estimated that standby and "always-on" consumption accounts for roughly 9–16% of domestic electricity. It will never be the biggest line in your retrofit plan — insulation and heating dwarf it — but it is the cheapest saving on this site to capture: the payback on switching something off at the wall is instant, the cost is zero, and the habit compounds across every device you own.

    The biggest standby offenders, ranked

    Not all standby is equal. The figures baked into this calculator are typical measured values, and the ranking matters more than the total — it tells you where the effort goes first:

    • Games consoles (10W): the standby champions nobody suspects. A console in "instant-on" mode can draw 10–15W around the clock so it can wake in seconds. Over 20 idle hours a day that is 73 kWh a year — £17.50 — for the privilege of skipping a 30-second boot. Switch instant-on off in the console's power settings (or the console off at the wall) and the standby draw collapses to under 1W.
    • Set-top boxes and streamers (8W): the old offender. Some boxes never truly sleep; they keep tuners, hard drives and network connections alive. If yours runs warm to the touch when "off", it is not off. A smart plug on a schedule — on for the evening, off overnight — fixes it without touching a cable.
    • Desktop PCs left in sleep (5W): sleep is not off. A desktop in sleep still draws 4–6W; hibernate draws ~1W and full shutdown ~0.5W. If the machine sits unused 20 hours a day, hibernate instead of sleep and pocket the difference.
    • Printers (4W), microwaves (3W), TVs (3W), smart speakers (3W): the long tail. Each is small; together they add up. The microwave's clock display costs about £6.30 a year — a clock you already have on your phone, paid for in electricity.
    • Laptop chargers (2W) and phone chargers (0.5W): genuinely trivial — a phone charger left plugged in costs about £1.05 a year. Unplug them anyway, not for the money but because a warm charger brick is a (very small) fire risk and the habit is free.

    Key definitions

    Standby power
    Electricity a device draws while "off" but still plugged in and able to wake — typically 0.5–15W.
    Vampire load
    The informal name for standby waste: power consumed by devices doing nothing useful.
    kWh (kilowatt-hour)
    The unit on your bill. 1 kWh = running a 1,000W appliance for one hour. Standby maths is watts × hours ÷ 1,000.
    Smart plug
    A plug with a timer/app switch that cuts power to a device (or a group) on a schedule — the lazy person's off-switch.

    What you must never switch off

    Before you go round flicking switches, the safety list — because some things are "on" for a reason:

    • Fridge and freezer: never. The food-safety risk dwarfs any saving, and the compressor cycling will just work harder afterwards.
    • Boiler and heating controls: modern boilers run frost protection and pump overrun cycles; isolating them can void warranties and, in a cold snap, burst pipes.
    • Broadband router: switching it off nightly saves ~£10 a year but can destabilise some lines (the exchange may interpret repeated drops as a faulty line and slow your speed) and kills anything that needs the network overnight — alarms, smart heating, security cameras.
    • Anything with a hard drive recording schedule (some set-top boxes) if you actually use the recordings — check the box's own eco settings instead.
    • Medical devices, stairlifts, alarms, and anything someone depends on. Obviously — but say it once, clearly.
    • Sky Q and similar: these download updates overnight; fully isolating them can mean missed recordings and long re-syncs. Use the box's built-in eco standby instead.

    The practical system: smart plugs and power strips

    Nobody sustains a nightly crawl behind the TV unit. The winning setup is a switched power strip or smart plug on the entertainment stack: TV, console, set-top box, soundbar and chargers all on one strip, one switch by the sofa, off at bedtime. A smart plug (£10–£15) adds scheduling — on at 6pm, off at midnight — so you never think about it again. The honest accounting: a smart plug itself draws about 1W (£2.10 a year), so only deploy them where they displace real standby load. One smart plug killing 25W of entertainment standby nets you £41.70 a year after its own consumption — payback in about four months. Ten smart plugs scattered around killing 1W chargers is just gadget theatre.

    For the desk, the same logic: PC, monitor, printer and desk lamp on one strip, switched off with the working day. For the kitchen: the microwave, kettle base (some have lit switches) and coffee machine on a strip you flick after breakfast. The pattern is always the same — group the standby, switch the group, automate the switch. Three strips and two smart plugs cover most homes for under £50, and the calculator above will tell you exactly what that £50 buys back each year.

    Beyond standby: the always-on audit

    Standby is only half the "invisible load" story. The other half is devices that are legitimately on but doing nothing: the second fridge in the garage humming away half-empty (£60–£100 a year to run), the old plasma TV still in the spare room, the heated towel rail on 24/7 in summer, the outdoor security light burning dusk-to-dawn instead of on a PIR sensor. Do a proper always-on walk-round once a year: stand at the meter, switch everything you can off, and watch the background draw. A typical home idles at 100–300W with "everything off"; if yours sits at 500W+, something is eating electricity around the clock and the meter will tell you what the bills never itemise.

    And the deepest cut of all is behavioural, not electrical: standby waste is a symptom of devices that default to "ready" instead of "off". When you buy replacements, check the standby rating (EU rules cap most devices at 0.5W standby, but consoles and boxes with network standby get higher allowances — read the spec sheet). Buy the 0.5W TV over the 3W one and the saving is locked in for the decade you own it, no willpower required.

    Where standby sits in your retrofit plan

    Be honest about scale: killing all standby might save £50 a year, while loft insulation saves £300 and a heat pump decision moves thousands. Standby belongs in Phase A — the quick wins of a whole-house plan (see the Retrofit Planner): zero-cost, instant-payback measures you do this weekend while the bigger projects go through quotes and installers. It also has a psychological function the big measures lack — it makes energy visible. The household that starts switching off at the wall is the household that notices the thermostat setting, the draught under the door, the radiator heating an empty room. Small wins train the attention that the big wins need.

    Key takeaways

    • Always size the measure for your home before spending — this calculator gives you the numbers.
    • Compare like-for-like quotes from certified installers and check live prices before you commit.
    • Fabric first: insulation and draught-proofing make every later upgrade (heat pumps, solar) cheaper and more effective.

    Frequently asked questions

    How much does standby power cost the average UK home?

    Typically £40-£70 a year, and over £100 in homes with games consoles and older set-top boxes. The four-device example above (TV, set-top box, console, printer at 20h/day standby) wastes £43.80 a year at 24p/kWh.

    Which device wastes the most on standby?

    Games consoles in instant-on mode (10-15W) and older set-top boxes (~8W) are the worst. A console left in instant-on 20 hours a day costs about £17.50 a year just to boot faster.

    Should I switch my TV off at the wall every night?

    Yes — it is completely safe for modern TVs, and the 3W standby (£6.30/year) disappears. Use a switched power strip so one flick kills the TV, console, box and soundbar together.

    Is it safe to switch everything off at the wall?

    No. Never isolate the fridge/freezer, boiler, medical devices, stairlifts or alarms. Routers are best left on (repeated drops can slow some broadband lines). Check the 'never switch off' list in the guide above.

    Do smart plugs actually save money?

    Only where they displace real standby load. A smart plug draws ~1W (£2.10/yr) itself, so one killing 25W of entertainment standby nets ~£42/yr — but ten plugs on 1W chargers just add cost.

    Does leaving a phone charger plugged in waste much?

    About 0.5W, roughly £1 a year. Unplug it anyway — the habit is free and a warm charger brick is a small fire risk — but do not expect it to move your bill.
